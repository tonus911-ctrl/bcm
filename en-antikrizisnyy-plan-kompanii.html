Company crisis plan: an action plan in 7 steps

A crisis plan is a short document that answers three questions: who decides, what we do, what we say. It does not need to be a thick binder. We go through a 7-step structure that works in a real crisis, and not just on paper.

What a crisis plan is and what it is not

A crisis is a situation in which the normal management system can no longer keep up: events move faster than approvals. A crisis plan is a set of decisions made in advance for this mode: who gets authority, which scenarios are covered, which first steps are taken without meetings.

What it is not. It is not a forecast: a crisis almost never follows the written scenario. It is not a rulebook for every case: a decision-making framework matters more than a thousand instructions. And it is not a document “for an inspection”. A plan like that is more dangerous than no plan at all, because it creates an illusion of readiness.

7 steps to a working plan

  1. Define crisis scenarios. Pick 5-7 that are realistic for your business, not 50: IT outage, an accident at a site, loss of a key supplier, an incident involving people, a public reputational crisis, licence withdrawal or regulator sanctions.
  2. Appoint the crisis team and deputies. By name, with decision rights and limits. Deputies are a must: crises like to strike during holidays.
  3. Describe activation triggers. Which signs trigger crisis mode, and who declares it. Half of the damage comes from late activation: everyone waits for things to “sort themselves out”.
  4. Prepare the first 24 hours. For each scenario, a checklist of first actions: isolate, assess, convene the crisis team, notify. In the first hours nobody thinks. People either follow a list or act chaotically.
  5. Prepare communications in advance. Message templates for employees, customers, partners and the media, written before the crisis (more in the article on crisis communications).
  6. Secure resources. Backup communication channels, access to cash for emergency spending, contacts of lawyers and contractors: everything you will not have time to look for in a crisis.
  7. Run a rehearsal. A 2-3 hour tabletop exercise on one scenario will reveal more gaps than a month of document approvals.

Crisis team: who decides

The working line-up: a crisis team leader with the right to make final decisions (usually the CEO or the COO) and the owners of key areas: IT, production/operations, finance, HR, communications, legal. The key point is authority agreed in advance: which spending the team can approve without the owner, and which decisions need escalation. In a crisis it is too late to bargain over authority.

Communications: what to say and to whom

Companies lose more from silence and contradictions than from the incident itself. The minimum set: a first message to employees (within hours, not days), a position for customers, answers to the 10 most unpleasant questions and one appointed voice of the company. All of this is organisational work covered by existing salaries: zero extra budget and the biggest effect on the outcome.

Rehearsals: why a plan is dead without them

The first rehearsal always exposes the same things: phone numbers are out of date, authority is unclear, message templates do not fit, and the “responsible person” learns about their role during the exercise. This is normal: that is what rehearsals are for. The minimum format: a tabletop simulation every six months, 2-3 hours, one scenario, a debrief straight afterwards. We run such simulations with a stopwatch and a report, including a tough ransomware scenario (how cyber exercises work).

Frequently asked questions

How does a crisis plan differ from a business continuity plan? A business continuity plan is about restoring processes and resources. A crisis plan is about management and decisions in the acute phase. In practice they are two layers of one system: the BCP answers “how do we keep working”, and the crisis plan answers “who decides what while we cannot work”.

How long should a normal plan be? A framework document of 10-15 pages plus one-page checklists for each scenario. Nobody opens anything thicker in a crisis.

Where do you start if you have one week? A crisis team with authority, a first-24-hours checklist for the two main scenarios and a message template for employees. This minimum alone changes the outcome radically.
