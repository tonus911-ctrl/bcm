BCP and DRP: the difference and how to link them

BCM, BCP, DRP, BIA, RTO, RPO are abbreviations that people mix up all the time, including in contracts and technical specifications. We explain each one and show the boundary between the business continuity plan and the disaster recovery plan. We also give a comparison table, a split of responsibilities and a minute-by-minute walk-through of one incident.

What the abbreviations stand for

BCP stands for Business Continuity Plan. It sets out who acts and in what order so that critical processes continue during a disruption. DRP stands for Disaster Recovery Plan, the plan for recovering IT systems and data after a failure. The BCP covers business processes and people, the DRP covers infrastructure; both are part of the business continuity management system (BCM).

How they are linked: nested levels

These approaches do not compete. They are circles nested inside one another. Each circle is narrower than the one around it and solves a narrower task.

  • Risk management is the widest circle. It identifies and reduces all kinds of risk the company faces, from currency risk to reputational risk.
  • BCM is the part of risk management that answers one question: how to keep working when something has already happened.
  • BCP is the main document within BCM. It covers the business as a whole: processes, people, suppliers, clients and communications.
  • DRP is the technical part, most often issued as an annex to the BCP. It covers servers, communication links, databases and backups.
In one sentence. The BCP answers the question “how will the company keep working”, and the DRP answers the question “how will we bring the systems back up”. The first question is wider and is asked earlier.

BCP and DRP: where the boundary lies

The boundary lies where technology ends and obligations to the client begin. The easiest way to see it is with three test questions.

Question one. What does the company do in the hours while the systems are being restored? If there is no answer, you have a DRP and no BCP. Even in the most optimistic scenario, recovery takes hours, and all that time the business has to keep working somehow: take orders on paper, serve clients in a backup environment, shift the workload to another office.

Question two. Who says what to clients, staff and the regulator? Crisis communications sit entirely in the BCP. No technical procedure covers them, and this is where the second, reputational, damage most often happens.

Question three. What if IT has nothing to do with it? A fire at the warehouse, no access to the office, the departure of a key employee, the failure of a sole supplier, blocked payments to a counterparty. By definition, a DRP does not cover such scenarios, yet they account for a significant share of real continuity disruptions.

The opposite case is rarer, but it happens too: a company writes a polished BCP with processes and communications, and it contains no technical targets at all. During an incident it turns out that the business expects recovery within two hours, while backups run once a day and physically cannot meet that target. That is why both documents are prepared together, and their target time frames must match.

Where to start linking them. The BCP structure and a docx template are covered on the business continuity plan page, and the IT recovery procedure on the disaster recovery plan (DRP) page. The target time frames in both documents come from one RTO and RPO matrix (in Russian).

Note that the DRP works first and last, while the BCP carries the whole middle part, when the company is actually operating without its systems. This middle part is usually missing in companies where only the IT department deals with continuity.

RTO, RPO, MTPD and MBCO

Four metrics where business expectations and technical capabilities most often diverge.

RTO, recovery time objective. How quickly a process or system must be back in operation. The business sets it on the basis of calculation; it should not be left to technical staff to set as an afterthought.

RPO, recovery point objective. How much data it is acceptable to lose. It is measured in time: an RPO of four hours means that in an outage the company is ready to lose the data from the last four hours of work. This metric directly determines how often backups are made and what the solution costs.

MTPD (maximum tolerable period of disruption). This is the period after which the damage from a stopped process becomes unacceptable for the company: it loses its licence, a key client or the ability to recover. The RTO must always be shorter than the MTPD, otherwise the plan is meaningless.

MBCO, minimum acceptable level of service. It sets how much service the company commits to keep up in crisis mode, which goes beyond a simple “working or not”. For example, handling at least 40% of the normal payment flow. This metric turns the plan from a yes-or-no switch into something you can manage.

Consistency check. Take any critical process and compare two figures: the RTO set by the business and the actual switchover time that the technical side delivers. If the second is longer than the first, the plan does not work, however many pages it has.

Russian terms: ONiVD and the ONiVD plan

In Russian regulatory practice, the same documents have other names. The Bank of Russia uses the term operational continuity and recovery (ONiVD), and the main document is called the ONiVD plan. In substance it is the same BCP, but with a mandatory set of sections, ties to technological processes with defined threshold values, and supervisory reporting.

The practical result is simple. If a company is supervised by the Bank of Russia, its current BCP is usually extended to meet the requirements of the regulation; there is no need to rewrite it from scratch. You add a list of technological processes, operational reliability targets, an incident recording procedure and a testing programme. A detailed review of the requirements and structure is in the article ONiVD and the ONiVD plan: Bank of Russia requirements, and a ready-made eleven-section plan framework in docx format is in the ONiVD plan sample (in Russian).

The reverse confusion also happens: a bank's contractors find a requirement to “provide an ONiVD plan” in their contract and do not understand what is wanted, although they already have a working business continuity plan under a different name.

Five common mistakes

Treating the DRP as enough. The most common mistake. The company orders a disaster recovery plan, ticks the “we have a plan” box and finds the gap only during a real incident.

Setting the RTO without calculation. The figure comes from general considerations instead of a business impact analysis. As a result, the company either overpays for redundancy of processes whose downtime is tolerable, or fails to provide redundancy for what is truly critical.

Different time frames in the BCP and DRP. The business wrote down two hours, the technical side delivers eight. The gap shows up at the very first test, if testing is done at all.

Forgetting non-IT scenarios. Loss of access to premises, loss of a key employee, supplier failure. These scenarios have no technical solution and need organisational arrangements made in advance.

A plan without an owner. The document exists, but no specific person is responsible for it, so it is not updated and goes out of date within a year.

Frequently asked questions

What does DRP stand for?

DRP stands for disaster recovery plan. It describes how to restore IT systems and data after a failure: in what order, how fast and with how much data loss. It is part of a wider business continuity plan, the BCP.

The difference between BC and DR plans

The business continuity plan, BCP, answers how the company will keep working until the systems are restored: people, premises, manual procedures, counterparties. The disaster recovery plan, DRP, answers how to bring the systems back. The first is wider, and the second sits inside it; the full comparison table is above.

BCP/DRP: does a company need both plans?

Yes, if the company has at least one critical IT system. Without a DRP, the business does not know when the systems will be back; without a BCP, it does not know what to do until then. For a small company, both plans can be sections of one document.

Is it a question for the DRP or the DRS?

A DRP is a disaster recovery plan, a document. A DRS, a disaster recovery site or disaster recovery system, is the backup site or system you switch to under this plan. The question “how fast will we recover” is for the plan; the question “where and on what” is for the site.

Who sets the RPO and how is it linked to the DRP?

The process owner sets the RPO in the BCP, and the RPO determines how often backups are made under the DRP.

What is BCM in plain words? Business continuity management. It is a system that decides in advance which processes must not stop, how long the company can survive without them and what exactly people do when a process stops.

Which comes first, the BCP or the DRP? First a business impact analysis, which shows the critical processes and the acceptable time frames. Then the BCP, which sets the requirements. The DRP is written to requirements that are already known; otherwise the technical time frames are pulled out of thin air.

How is BCM linked to information security? Information security prevents and detects an attack; BCM keeps the company working if the attack succeeds. These are neighbouring functions with shared scenarios. More in the article BCM and data protection.

Does a small company need a BCP? Yes, but only a few pages long. A small business has few critical processes, and a plan for them takes one working day to write. Having no plan costs more.

What is a BCP stress test? A test of the plan under near-extreme conditions: several dependencies fail at once, some people are unavailable and time is short. The aim is to find the point where the plan breaks; passing the test is not the goal.
