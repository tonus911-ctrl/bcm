KRI: what it is in plain language and how it differs from KPI

We explain in plain language what a KRI is, how it differs from a KPI (with two examples), what a working indicator should look like and how thresholds are set.

Updated: 17 August 2026 · Author: Evgeny Telenkov · ≈ 4 min read

A KRI (key risk indicator) is a leading indicator that warns of a rising risk before the risk occurs. A KPI (key performance indicator) is a lagging indicator that measures a result already achieved. A KRI answers the question “what could happen”, a KPI answers “what happened”. Both have a threshold, but the response to a KRI threshold prevents the event, while the response to a KPI threshold records it.

Both indicators look similar: a number and a threshold. The easiest way to see the difference is through examples.

The difference, by example

Take an IT department. KPI: service availability for the month was 99.2 per cent. This is a fact. It has already happened, and nothing can change it. KRI: the share of servers whose software has not been updated for more than ninety days has risen to 18 per cent. This is not an incident yet, but the probability of one has grown, and there is still time to respond.

The same principle applies in procurement. KPI: three deliveries failed in the quarter. KRI: 40 per cent of critical items have only one supplier. The second indicator warns of the risk covered in the article loss of a supplier.

What a working KRI should look like

  • Leading. It changes before the event, not at the same time. The number of incidents per month is a KPI, whatever you call it.
  • With a threshold and a recipient. The indicator has a value at which someone must act. Without a threshold it is just a number in a report.
  • Linked to a specific risk in the register. It is tied to a risk that has an owner, and not to a process in general. See risk management system.
  • Calculated automatically. An indicator collected by hand once a quarter comes too late to warn anyone.
  • Few in number. Five to seven indicators at management board level and two or three per business area. Nobody reads a long list.

How thresholds are set

A threshold is neither an average value nor a wish. It is derived from the limit the company has set for itself, that is, from its risk appetite. Suppose acceptable losses from downtime are capped at an annual amount, and one day of downtime has a known cost. This directly gives the maximum number of days, and therefore the threshold values of the indicators that lead to downtime.

A practical technique is to use two levels. A yellow threshold means “investigate and report”; a red one means “act immediately and escalate”. Who receives the signal at each level is set by the three lines of defence model.

How to calculate the cost from which thresholds are derived is covered in cost of one day of downtime.

How it is written: KRI, its Cyrillic form and the full Russian term

The term appears in three spellings, and all three mean the same thing: KRI, the English abbreviation of key risk indicator; the same abbreviation typed in Cyrillic letters on a Russian keyboard layout; and the full Russian phrase for “key risk indicator”. In company documents it is better to choose one spelling and stick to it. Otherwise, searches across the register and reports stop finding half of the rows.

Frequently asked questions

KRI: what it is in plain language

A KRI, or key risk indicator, is a number that shows that the probability of an unwanted event is rising before the event happens. For example, the share of overdue customer payments or the number of days since the last backup. The indicator has a threshold, and when it is crossed, everyone knows in advance who does what.

Where does the data come from? From systems that already run: accounting, service desk, HR. The data export is set up once; after that the indicator updates without manual collection.
