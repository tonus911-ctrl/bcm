Operational resilience: what it is and how it differs from continuity

The ability to keep delivering critical services during disruptions, and not only to recover after them. What lies behind it and what a Russian company should do.

Updated: 17 August 2026 · Author: Evgeny Telenkov · ≈ 5 min read
On this page
How it differs from business continuity What it consists of in practice How it relates to Russian requirements Frequently asked questions

Operational resilience is a company's ability to keep delivering critical services during disruptions, and not only to recover after them. The term came from the requirements of financial regulators and is gradually spreading to other industries.

How it differs from business continuity

Continuity management answers the question of how a company gets back to work after a stoppage. Operational resilience asks a harder question: how to make sure a service is never disrupted for longer than is tolerable, even when part of the system has failed.

This leads to three practical differences. First, the starting point is the service as the customer sees it, not a company process. Second, planning starts from the assumption that a disruption will definitely happen, not from an attempt to prevent it. Third, an impact tolerance is set: the limit beyond which harm to customers and the market is considered unacceptable.

The basic concepts of continuity are covered in business continuity, and the difference between management levels in BCM, BCP and DRP.

What it consists of in practice

  • A list of important services. These are the services the company delivers to the outside world, not its processes. There are usually only a few, from five to fifteen.
  • A map of what the service depends on. People, systems, sites, suppliers, data. This extends the critical process register.
  • Impact tolerance. The maximum duration and scale of disruption that the company considers tolerable for the customer.
  • Scenario testing. Start from “we assume it has happened” instead of “could this happen”. See the continuity stress test.
  • Third-party dependencies. The weakest part for most companies. See loss of a supplier.

How it relates to Russian requirements

Russian regulation has no direct equivalent of the term, but the logic is there. The Bank of Russia's requirements for operational continuity and recovery (ONiVD) set a mandatory minimum for recovery times and notification procedures. See ONiVD and the ONiVD plan. The ISO 22301 standard provides the management framework.

For a company outside the financial sector, the practical conclusion is simple. There is no need to build a separate system for a new term. It is enough to add the customer's view to your existing work and set an impact tolerance for each important service.

Frequently asked questions

Operational resilience: definition

Operational resilience is a company's ability to keep delivering critical services through any disruption, including unforeseen ones, and within a tolerable level of disruption set in advance. The term comes from banking regulation and is broader than continuity: it covers adaptation as well as recovery.

Operational resilience and operational continuity: differences and similarities

Similarities: both are about the ability to operate during a disruption, and both rely on critical processes and recovery time. Differences: continuity responds to known scenarios with plans, while resilience must also withstand unknown ones. Continuity is measured by recovery time; resilience is measured by the tolerable level of service disruption for the customer.

Are operational resilience plans and operational continuity plans the same thing

No, but the second is part of the first. A continuity plan describes how to restore a process. An operational resilience system adds a map of critical customer services, tolerable disruption thresholds for each one, scenario testing including extreme scenarios, and reporting to the board.

Are operational resilience and business continuity the same thing? No. Continuity answers how to recover after a stoppage. Resilience requires that a service is not disrupted for longer than is tolerable, and it plans on the assumption that a disruption will definitely happen.

Is it mandatory in Russia? Not as a separate requirement. Organisations supervised by the Bank of Russia must meet a minimum for operational continuity, and the logic itself is useful for any company.

Where do you start? List the most important services for customers and, for each one, set the maximum tolerable duration of disruption. Then check what the service depends on.
