Testing your company's crisis readiness: methods and a checklist

A plan on paper and real readiness are different things. The only way to know whether the business will survive a serious disruption is to test it before the disruption happens. We look at readiness testing methods, from tabletop exercises to a full stress test.

Published: 24 July 2026 · Author: Evgeny Telenkov · ≈ 6 min read

Why test readiness

In our experience, most business continuity plans do not survive first contact with reality: contacts are out of date, the responsible person has left, the backup will not restore, and decisions in the first hours are made by someone without the authority to make them. Readiness testing reveals this in advance, when fixing it is cheap. For companies that work to ISO 22301, regular testing is a direct requirement of the standard.

Testing methods: from simple to in-depth

  • Tabletop exercises. The management team works through a scenario “at the table”: who finds out first, who decides, what we tell clients. It takes 3–4 hours and reveals most organisational gaps. More in the article on business continuity exercises.
  • Stress simulation. The team goes through a realistic scenario (most often a ransomware attack) with timed inputs: decisions, communications, recovery. This is the continuity stress test format with an external facilitator.
  • Technical tests. Actual restoration of systems from backups, with the time measured. This is part of the disaster recovery plan (DRP).
  • Full stress test. A diagnostic review of the continuity system, plus a simulation, plus a calculation of downtime economics. It gives the fullest “snapshot” of readiness in a short time.

For two common scenarios there is a ready-made format, the scenario readiness check: drone strike and ransomware, five weeks and findings for the owner.

What to measure

  1. Recovery time. How long recovery will actually take, compared with the target RTO.
  2. Data loss (RPO). How much data will be lost when you roll back to the last backup.
  3. Money. The cost of one day of downtime and the share of revenue at risk: the main language for talking to senior management.
  4. Quality of decisions. Speed, gaps in authority, readiness of communications, as recorded by an observer.
In practice: our 2-day continuity stress test combines all the levels: a day of diagnostics (100+ questions), a day of ransomware stress simulation with the leadership team, and a report for the CEO with the damage in money, the weak points and a 90-day plan.

Checklist of first steps

  1. Choose one scenario, the most painful one for your industry (ransomware suits almost everyone).
  2. Bring together the management team and the owners of key functions: 6–12 people.
  3. Run a tabletop review: 3–4 hours on the scenario, with an observer recording the gaps.
  4. Calculate the cost of one day of downtime, even roughly, based on revenue.
  5. Record the findings and assign owners for the fixes, with deadlines.
  6. Repeat in six months to a year: without regular practice, readiness fades.

Frequently asked questions

Where do you start readiness testing?

With a tabletop review of one scenario with the management team: ransomware or the loss of a key supplier. It takes 3–4 hours and exposes the main gaps.

How often should readiness be tested?

At least once a year with one scenario; for critical IT systems, more often. After significant changes, an unscheduled check.

What should you measure in testing?

Actual recovery time against the target RTO, acceptable data loss (RPO) and the cost of one day of downtime in money. Plus the quality of decisions and communications.
